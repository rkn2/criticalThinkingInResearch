# Week 3 — What Don't You Know?

**Date:** September 17, 2026
**Students:** Carol, Marcio, Yishuang
**Duration:** 1 hour

## What we covered

Revisited the three-step sentences from Week 2. Named Step 2 as the
research question. Worked through what makes a research question real
(three tests: names what you don't know, has more than one possible
answer, evidence could change the answer). Showed common problems
(too broad, foregone conclusion, tool-centric, restating Step 1).

Workshop: each student sharpened their Step 2 into a standalone
research question.

Did NOT get to hypotheses, falsifiability, or Chamberlin. The research
questions took the full session.

## What happened

Research questions came out fuzzy. Carol and Marcio (the Brazilians)
mostly wrote hypotheses with question marks on the end — proposed
answers phrased as questions, not genuine scientific questions that
define what they don't know. The distinction between "a question that
has a range of possible answers" and "a statement I already believe,
phrased as a question" is not clear yet.

Yishuang's status not noted separately.

## For next time

1. **Start by revisiting the research question problem.** The questions
   they wrote today aren't ready yet. Don't move on until they can
   write a question that passes all three tests.

2. **Then introduce hypotheses as the next move.** The question-vs-
   hypothesis distinction is the key lesson — a hypothesis is a
   proposed *answer*, not a question with a question mark. The
   Brazilians' output today is actually closer to hypotheses than to
   questions, so naming that explicitly ("what you wrote is a
   hypothesis, not a question — now write the question it answers")
   may be the unlock.

3. **Then scope.** Once they have real questions and real hypotheses,
   scope defines which hypotheses their evidence can actually test.

4. **Chamberlin** on multiple working hypotheses fits naturally after
   they have at least one hypothesis each.
