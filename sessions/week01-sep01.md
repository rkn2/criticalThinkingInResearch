## Block 2: Concept — Tasks vs. questions, and the three-step formula (15 min)

### The core distinction

"I'm using ABAQUS to model walls" is a task.
"Can fire-cut joist retrofits resist EF3 uplift without compromising
reversibility?" is a question.

A task has no wrong answer. A question can be wrong — and that's what makes
it research.

### The Craft of Research formula (3.4.1–3.4.3)

Walk through the three steps with the group:

1. **Name your topic** (3.4.1): "I am studying ___."
   Fill in the blank with action nouns — causes, influence, development,
   performance — not static labels.

2. **Add your question** (3.4.2): "...because I want to find out ___."
   This is what you don't know. It turns a topic into a question.

3. **Answer So What?** (3.4.3): "...in order to help my reader understand ___."
   This is the significance — why anyone outside your lab should care.

**Worked example (use the Kallas & Napolitano blast paper):**
1. I am studying the *influence* of 3D geometric attributes on blast
   *vulnerability* of historic masonry buildings...
2. ...because I want to find out whether geometric features extracted from
   3D models *predict damage better* than traditional field-survey
   attributes...
3. ...in order to help post-disaster responders understand which buildings
   to prioritize *without* needing to measure every facade by hand.

### Instrumental vs. expressive behavior (from Unwritten Rules, Ch. 3)

Quick aside: a student sitting in the library reading six key texts for
their area is doing *instrumental* work — it moves them toward the goal. A
student reading the same irrelevant page over and over in a state of panic is
doing *expressive* work — it looks like effort but produces nothing.

The three-step formula is a diagnostic: if you can fill in all three blanks,
your work is instrumental. If you can only fill in step 1, you might be doing
expressive work — busy, but not aimed.

## Block 3: Workshop — Write your three-step sentence (20 min)

Each student writes their own version of:

> I am studying ___
> because I want to find out ___
> in order to help my reader understand ___.

Rules:
- Maximum 3 research questions per student, each with all three steps.
- No tool names in step 1 or step 3.
- Step 2 must be something you genuinely don't know yet — not a foregone
  conclusion.
- Step 3 must name a reader who is not your advisor. Who else cares?

Students write for 10 minutes, then each reads their best one aloud.

**Becca's role:** Listen for steps that collapse — a step 2 that's really just
restating step 1 ("because I want to find out about the influence of..."
means you don't have a question yet). Push on step 3 especially — most
students will struggle to name a reader.

## Block 4: Career paths — Where is the PhD taking you? (15 min)

### Why talk about this now?

The three-step formula ends with "in order to help my reader understand."
Who is your reader depends on where you end up. Career path shapes how you
think about significance, audience, and what counts as a result.

### Live discussion: comparison table (put on screen)

| | Tenure-line | Teaching prof | Industry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary job** | Research + grants + teaching + service | Teaching + curriculum + mentoring | Applied R&D or practice |
| **Evaluated on** | Publications, funding, impact | Teaching quality, student outcomes | Deliverables, clients, products |
| **Your "reader"** | Research community (journals) | Students and practitioners | Clients, agencies, stakeholders |
| **Build during PhD** | Papers, conference talks, grant experience | Teaching portfolio, pedagogy, mentoring | Technical skills, internships, applied work |
| **Tradeoff** | Freedom vs. pressure (tenure clock) | Stability vs. less research autonomy | Pay/balance vs. less intellectual freedom |

### Discussion prompts (pick 2, let conversation flow)

1. Which of these three paths interests you right now, and why?
2. Look at your step 3 from the workshop — which row's "reader" does it
   sound like? Would it change if you were aiming for a different path?
3. What should you be doing *during the PhD* to keep multiple paths open?

### Reference: detailed path descriptions (handout, not presented live)

**1. Tenure-line faculty (research professor)**

What it is: Research + teaching + grants + service. You run a lab, advise
students, write proposals, publish papers, and serve on committees. You are
evaluated primarily on research productivity and funding.

What it takes during the PhD:
- Multiple journal publications (first-author)
- Conference presentations and visibility in your research community
- Grant-writing experience (even if just helping your advisor)
- Teaching experience (TA, guest lectures)
- A clear, fundable research agenda that extends beyond the dissertation

The reality: Feibelman calls the assistant professor path "most of the
disadvantages and few of the benefits of a tenured position." You start from
scratch — new courses, new lab, no funding yet — and have 5–7 years to earn
tenure. The Unwritten Rules describes this as the "empire builder" route.

The payoff: Intellectual freedom, job security after tenure, the ability to
shape a field.

**3. Industry (engineering firms, tech, government labs, consulting)**

What it is: Applied research or practice in private industry, government
agencies (FEMA, NIST, DOE national labs), or consulting firms. Your work
solves specific problems for specific clients or stakeholders.

What it takes during the PhD:
- Strong technical skills and a portfolio of applied work
- Ability to communicate results to non-academic audiences
- Publications help but are not required — demonstrated capability matters more
- Internship or co-op experience is a significant advantage
- Network in the industry you're targeting

The reality: Feibelman notes that the job description is "relatively simple"
compared to academia — you're expected to produce results, and your employer
provides resources. Better pay, more reasonable hours, less autonomy. The
Unwritten Rules warns that if your results are proprietary, you won't build
the same research reputation.

The payoff: Real-world impact. You see your work deployed. Better
work-life balance and compensation.
