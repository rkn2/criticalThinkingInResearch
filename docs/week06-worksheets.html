Telling a Research Story

Putting everything you have built into an order an audience can follow

Invert the pyramid

Papers teach us to establish credibility first: background, then methods, then, finally, the result. A listener stops paying attention long before they get there. Start with the result and why it matters, then fill in the details. (After Somerville & Hassol 2011, via Shorr)

Say what the detail means

The detailWhat it means
“Excitation was restricted to a femtoliter volume.”“We eliminated a major source of noise.”
“I’m building an annotated corpus.”“I’m teaching a computer what to look for.”
“I ran 200 nonlinear analyses.”? If you can’t fill this in, the analyses are what you did, not what you found.

Research fits a story template

Goal: the big problem you’re trying to solve
Obstacle: why it’s hard, and why it hasn’t been done
Approach: what you do that gets past the obstacle
Result: what you found (so far)
Benefit: how that result moves the original goal forward

The elements are a checklist. A missing one is a hole in your talk.

“We want ___, but ___”

Chain the obstacles; each one you solve becomes the next, narrower goal. Radiolab on Fritz Haber: the world couldn’t feed a growing population, but plants need nitrogen, but the nitrogen in the air isn’t in a form plants can use, but it clings tightly to itself. Haber used temperature and pressure to turn it into ammonia, and that helps feed billions of people.

The U-shape, and the broken path

A talk starts at the Goal, goes down through the Obstacles, across your Approach, up through your Results, and finishes at the Benefit. The most common failure stays on the bottom row: all Approach, with no Goal it serves and no Benefit it delivers. That is the “here’s what I did” talk instead of the “here’s what I learned” talk.

Adapted from Ardon Shorr, “Telling Research Stories,” Princeton (2020). The Week 7 packet walks the U one stop at a time.

Lead With What You Learned

Turning what you did into what it means

Warm-up: Write your current key finding in one sentence. Read it to yourself. Is this something you learned, or something you did?

learneddid

Say what your details mean. List technical details you’d put in a talk: a method, a dataset, a number of runs, a model choice. Then say what each one means for someone who doesn’t do your work.

Invert your pyramid. Write the first two sentences of a talk on your work: the result, then why it matters. Background comes later.

Check before you move on

  • Any row you couldn’t fill in on the right is something you did, not something you found. Mark it.
  • Your opening sentences would make sense to someone who stopped listening after them.
  • If your warm-up sentence was “did,” rewrite it as “learned” using the right-hand column.
