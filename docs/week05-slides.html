Part 1: The Boundary

Scope Is a Design Decision

Scope is not a limitation you apologize for. It is the boundary around what your evidence can actually test.

Indefensible

Claiming your retrofit works against EF5 tornadoes when your loading represents EF2–3.

That isn’t ambitious. A committee member will find the gap in one question.

Imprecise

Claiming you validated a method when you replicated a published result.

That isn’t generous. It hides what you actually showed.

As paradoxical as it seems, you make your argument stronger and more credible by modestly acknowledging its limits… Limit your claims to what your argument can actually support by qualifying their scope and certainty. — Booth et al., The Craft of Research, §8.3, p. 129

Your Question and Your Hypothesis Have Different Boundaries

The question is what you don’t know, and it has more than one possible answer. The hypothesis is the answer you propose, under the conditions your evidence can actually test. The second is usually smaller.

Research question

“What are the structural limits of retrofitting historic URM against tornado loading, and how do those limits interact with preservation constraints?”

Covers historic URM, any retrofit, and tornado loading. Set by the gap and by who needs the answer: owners and engineers of historic buildings.

Hypothesis

On historic lime-mortar brick, the bond between a surface-mounted composite strip and the wall, not the strip’s own strength, limits what the retrofit can deliver. So a lighter intervention is structurally optimal, which favors preservation.

Covers one retrofit family on low-strength lime-mortar brick. Set by the material data and what the model represents.

The difference (other retrofit families, stronger masonry, loading the model doesn’t represent) is not a weakness. Say it out loud: that is where your answer stops and future work starts.

Scoping Your Hypothesis

Ask: under what conditions can my evidence actually tell hypothesis A apart from B and C? That’s your scope.

In

Low-strength lime-mortar brick walls

Surface-mounted composite strips

The loading range my load model represents

Out, and why

Modern cement-mortar masonry, because the bond limit I’m testing shows up on low-strength masonry, and my material data is lime mortar.

Other retrofit families, because my hypothesis is about strip-to-wall bond, and other retrofits don’t depend on it.

Loading beyond my load model, because past that range I have no basis for the pressures on the wall.

Every “out” needs a because: the material data I have, the assumption my model makes, the loading my code provisions cover. “I didn’t have time” is not a reason. “The model assumes X, which stops holding past Y” is.

Your Scope Paragraph

Four parts. One short paragraph. No tool names unless the tool sets the boundary.

1. In: My work covers  
2. Out: It does not cover  
3. Why here: The boundary is here because  
4. What sets it: The evidence or assumption behind that boundary is  

Write it once for your question and once for your hypothesis, or as a table (next slide).

Test: could a committee member push on any one of your boundaries and find you don’t have a reason? Then that boundary isn’t designed yet.

In the Committee Meeting

Your Boundary Is Where You Show What You Know

When a committee member asks about something past your line, they usually aren’t asking you to do it. They are checking whether you understand what’s on the other side.

Knows only the inside

“That’s out of scope.”

The line looks like where you stopped looking.

Knows both sides

“Past that line, I’d expect X to change, because Y. That’s exactly why I drew it there.”

The line looks like a decision you made on purpose.

You don’t have to have done it. You have to understand it. Knowing the other side is what makes your line credible.

Talk About It Without Signing Up for It

Describe the other side like an expert. Don’t promise to go there.

Shows you can think

Predict: “I’d expect… because…”

Mechanism: “What changes past that point is…”

Literature: “Others who looked at that found…”

Cost: “Answering it would take…”

Signs you up for work

“I could add that.”

“That should be easy to include.”

“I’ll look into running that.”

“I haven’t had time yet.”

Speak about the other side in the conditional: what would happen, what it would take. That shows knowledge without making a commitment.

What That Sounds Like

“How would your strips do on a cement-mortar wall?”

1. Reason past the line: what you’d expect there, and why.
2. Anchor the line: why your work stops here.
3. Name the cost: what it would take to cross, and where that belongs.
Reason “I’d expect bond to matter less. On stronger masonry the strip has a better substrate to hold onto, so the strip’s own strength would start to govern, closer to what the design equations assume. Anchor That’s why I scoped to lime mortar: the bond limit I’m testing shows up on low-strength masonry, and that’s the material data I have. Cost Cement mortar would need its own bond tests. It’s a natural next study.”

Most of that answer is about the part you didn’t study. That is what shows you know your work.

When It Really Is a Request for More Work

Sometimes a committee member isn’t testing you. “You should also…” or “Before you defend, I’d want to see…” is a proposal to change your project.

In the room

Engage with the idea the same way: reason about it, anchor your line, name the cost. Then: “Let me think about how that fits, and follow up with my advisor.”

Don’t agree to new work on the spot.

After the meeting

Decide with your advisor whether it belongs in this dissertation, then confirm with the committee member in writing.

Scope gets negotiated, not conceded.

And if the push shows your line has no reason behind it, the committee is right and the line moves. That’s why you design your scope now.

Workshop: What Will You Defend?

Picture a committee member reading your scope paragraph. Write three lines.

1. My claim: My work shows  
2. The push: The boundary a committee member is most likely to question is  
3. My answer: When they do, I will say  

Write.

Hot seat: read us your claim. We play committee and ask about what’s past your line. Show us you understand the other side, without signing up to go there. If you can’t give a reason for a boundary, it goes back into your scope paragraph.
