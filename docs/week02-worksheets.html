Why Does Anyone Care?

Turning topics into questions worth asking

Tasks vs. questions

Everything you do is either a task or aimed at answering a question. Both are real, but you need to know which is which.

TaskSomething you do. It has steps and a completion state, but no wrong answer.
“I’m using ABAQUS to model walls.”
QuestionSomething you’re trying to find out. It can be wrong, and that’s what makes it research.
“Can fire-cut joist retrofits resist EF3 uplift without compromising reversibility?”

The three-step formula

  1. I am studying ___ names your topic; use action nouns (causes, influence, performance)
  2. because I want to find out ___ turns it into a question: what you don’t know
  3. in order to help my reader understand ___ answers “So what?” for someone outside your lab

Booth et al., The Craft of Research, 3.4.1–3.4.3, pp. 44–45

Worked example

Kallas & Napolitano (2025), Int. J. Disaster Risk Reduction
  1. I am studying the influence of 3D geometric attributes on blast vulnerability of historic masonry buildings…
  2. because I want to find out whether geometric features extracted from 3D models predict damage better than traditional field-survey attributes…
  3. in order to help post-disaster responders understand which buildings to prioritize without needing to measure every facade by hand.

Instrumental vs. expressive

InstrumentalBehavior that moves you toward your goal. A student reading six key texts for their area.
ExpressiveBehavior that demonstrates effort but produces nothing. A student reading the same irrelevant page over and over in a state of panic.

The three-step formula is a diagnostic. If you can fill in all three blanks, your work is instrumental. If you can only fill in Step 1, you might be busy, but not aimed. (Rugg & Petre, The Unwritten Rules of PhD Research, Ch. 3)

What Did You Do, and What Did It Answer?

Task (something you did)Question it serves (something you’re trying to find out)

Check before you move on

  • Does every entry in the right column have a possible wrong answer? If not, it is another task.
  • How many rows say “none yet”? A task with no question isn’t wrong, but you should know which it is.
  • Do several tasks point to the same question? That question is probably your real one.
