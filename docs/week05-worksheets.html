Scope: What Are You Not Claiming?

Drawing the boundary around your question and your hypothesis, and getting ready to defend it

Scope is a design decision. It is not a limitation you apologize for. It is the boundary around what your evidence can actually test.

IndefensibleClaiming your retrofit works against EF5 tornadoes when your loading represents EF2–3. That isn’t ambitious. A committee member will find the gap in one question.
ImpreciseClaiming you validated a method when you replicated a published result. That isn’t generous. It hides what you actually showed.
As paradoxical as it seems, you make your argument stronger and more credible by modestly acknowledging its limits… — Booth et al., The Craft of Research, §8.3, p. 129

Your question and your hypothesis have different boundaries

Question scope comes from the gap and from who needs the answer. Hypothesis scope comes from where your evidence can tell your answer apart from the competing one. The second is usually smaller. The difference between them is not a weakness: it is where your answer stops and future work starts.

The scope paragraph: four parts

  1. In: My work covers ___
  2. Out: It does not cover ___
  3. Why here: The boundary is here because ___
  4. What sets it: The evidence or assumption behind that boundary is ___

Every “out” needs a because: the material data you have, the assumption your model makes, the loading your code provisions cover. “I didn’t have time” is not a reason. “The model assumes X, which stops holding past Y” is.

In the committee meeting

When a committee member asks about something past your line, they usually aren’t asking you to do it. They are checking whether you understand what’s on the other side. Answer in three moves: reason past the line, anchor the line, name the cost. You don’t have to have done it. You have to understand it.

Check before you move on

  • Every “out” has a because, and none of them is “I didn’t have time.”
  • The hypothesis columns are narrower than the question column.
  • Could a committee member push on any one boundary and find you don’t have a reason? Then that boundary isn’t designed yet.

Week 5 · Worksheet 3

Answer the Hardest One

Show you know the other side of your line without signing up to go there

Shows you can thinkPredict: “I’d expect… because…” Mechanism: “What changes past that point is…” Literature: “Others who looked at that found…” Cost: “Answering it would take…”
Signs you up for work“I could add that.” “That should be easy to include.” “I’ll look into running that.” “I haven’t had time yet.”
Example: “How would your strips do on a cement-mortar wall?” Reason: I’d expect bond to matter less. On stronger masonry the strip has a better substrate to hold onto, so the strip’s own strength would start to govern. Anchor: That’s why I scoped to lime mortar: the bond limit I’m testing shows up on low-strength masonry, and that’s the material data I have. Cost: Cement mortar would need its own bond tests. It’s a natural next study, but it is not part of this dissertation.

The starred question from your map.

Reason past the line what you’d expect there, and why

When it really is a request for more work (“You should also…”, “Before you defend, I’d want to see…”): engage the same way, then say “Let me think about how that fits, and follow up with my advisor.” Don’t agree to new work in the room. Which of your five might be one, and what would you say?

Check before you move on

  • Most of your answer is about the part you didn’t study. That is what shows you know your work.
  • No sentence in it promises to do the work.
  • If the push showed your line has no reason behind it, the line moves. Go back to Worksheet 1.
