Warm-up: Read It Back

Pull up the research question you wrote last week. Read it aloud, exactly as written.

Everyone else: as you listen, ask yourself one thing —

Does this sentence ask something, or does it already contain an answer?

Part 1: Finish the Question

A Hypothesis With a Question Mark

These two sentences both end in “?”. Only one of them is a research question.

Looks like a question

“Does the bond between the composite strip and lime-mortar brick govern the capacity of surface-mounted retrofits?”

You already believe the answer (bond). You’ve named the mechanism and you’re asking for “yes.” This is a hypothesis with a question mark on the end.

Is a question

“What governs the capacity of surface-mounted retrofits on historic lime-mortar brick?”

Bond is one possible answer. So is strip strength. So is the masonry itself. You genuinely don’t know which yet.

How to Tell Which One You Wrote

  • Can it be answered “yes” or “no”? Yes/no questions are usually a single hypothesis being checked.
  • Do you already expect “yes”? Then you’ve written your prediction, not your uncertainty.
  • Does the question name the answer? A specific mechanism, variable, or direction (“does X increase Y?”) that you already think is right.
If any of these is true, you wrote a hypothesis.
That’s not a failure — you’ll need it in twenty minutes. It just isn’t the question yet.

A Good Question Has More Than One Answer

A good research question reduces the problem space in an area. This means that the answer, whatever it is, eliminates one set of plausible possibilities. — Rugg & Petre, The Unwritten Rules of PhD Research, p. 146
It is a good habit to work out the possible answers to your research question before you start your data collection… — Rugg & Petre, p. 147

Their first test of a research question: “Are you trying to find something out, rather than prove something?” (p. 148)

The Move: Work Backward

What you wrote
“Does bond govern capacity?”
→
Keep it
That’s your hypothesis: bond governs.
→
Write the question it answers
“What governs capacity?”

Check: list two other possible answers to your new question. If you can’t think of any, the question is still too narrow — it’s your hypothesis again in different words.

Workshop: Question or Hypothesis?

Take what you wrote last week.

  • Label it: question or hypothesis? Use the three checks.
  • If it’s a hypothesis, keep it on the page and write the question it answers above it.
  • List three possible answers to your question — your favorite plus two real alternatives.
  • Re-run last week’s tests: names what you don’t know? More than one possible answer? Could evidence change the answer?

Write for 8 minutes, then each reads their question and three answers aloud.

Part 2: The Hypothesis

What Is a Hypothesis?

A research question opens a door. A hypothesis walks through it.

Research question

What you’re trying to find out.

It defines the territory. It has a range of possible answers. You don’t know which one is right.

Hypothesis

A proposed answer — specific and testable.

It picks one door, walks through it, and says: “I think this is what’s on the other side.” It can be wrong.

You just listed three possible answers. Each one is a candidate hypothesis — what C. S. Peirce called “a hypothesis on probation.” (Booth et al., The Craft of Research, p. 65)

Worked Example

Research question

What governs the capacity of surface-mounted retrofits on historic lime-mortar brick, and how does that limit interact with preservation constraints?

Possible answers
  • A. The bond between strip and wall governs — so a lighter intervention is structurally optimal, which favors preservation.
  • B. The strip’s own strength governs — more fibre, proportionally more capacity.
  • C. The masonry itself fails first — the retrofit barely engages.

Pick one and commit: A is the hypothesis. B and C are its competitors.

What Would Make You Wrong?

If you can’t answer this, you don’t have a hypothesis — you have a hope.

The hypothesis holds if

The ACI reinforcement efficiency factor collapses to its floor on low-strength masonry, AND the FE model shows that connection layout — not strip strength — governs capacity.

The hypothesis fails if

κv does not collapse on low-f′m masonry, OR the FE model shows that adding more fibre proportionally increases capacity.

Don’t set out to collect data in support of your belief. Ask yourself: ‘How could I tell if my belief was wrong?’ If you can’t answer that question, then you’re doing politics, not research. — Rugg & Petre, The Unwritten Rules of PhD Research, p. 215

These Are Not Hypotheses

Unfalsifiable

“My model will provide useful results.”

What result would make you say it didn’t?

A task in disguise

“I will use ABAQUS to simulate the wall.”

That’s a plan. What do you expect to find?

Foregone conclusion

“The retrofitted wall will perform better.”

Of course it does. What is the interesting claim?

Too vague

“Building characteristics affect damage.”

Which characteristics? Which damage? Under what conditions?

Why You Need More Than One

The moment one has offered an original explanation for a phenomenon which seems satisfactory, that moment affection for his intellectual child springs into existence… There is an unconscious selection and magnifying of the phenomena that fall into harmony with the theory and support it, and an unconscious neglect of those that fail of coincidence. — T. C. Chamberlin, Science (1890)

If you hold only one hypothesis, you design experiments to confirm it. If you hold two competing hypotheses, you design experiments to distinguish between them. That is a different — and better — kind of research.

Discuss: What is your “intellectual child”? What result would you be tempted to explain away?

Workshop: Propose an Answer

Start from your question and its three possible answers.

  • Pick one as your hypothesis — a proposed answer, specific enough to be wrong.
  • Write what would make you wrong — what evidence would kill it?
  • Pick a competing hypothesis from your other two answers.
  • Name one experiment that distinguishes them — a result that would come out one way if you’re right and another way if the competitor is.

Write for 10 minutes. Then each reads theirs aloud.

Part 3: The Boundary

Scope Is a Design Decision

Scope is not a limitation you apologize for. It is the boundary around what your evidence can actually test.

Indefensible

Claiming your retrofit works against EF5 tornadoes when your loading represents EF2–3.

That isn’t ambitious. A committee member will find the gap in one question.

Imprecise

Claiming you validated a method when you replicated a published result.

That isn’t generous. It hides what you actually showed.

As paradoxical as it seems, you make your argument stronger and more credible by modestly acknowledging its limits… Limit your claims to what your argument can actually support by qualifying their scope and certainty. — Booth et al., The Craft of Research, §8.3, p. 129

Scope Comes From Your Hypothesis

Ask: under what conditions can my evidence actually tell hypothesis A apart from B and C? That’s your scope.

In

Low-strength lime-mortar brick walls. Surface-mounted composite strips. The loading range my load model represents.

Out

Modern cement-mortar masonry. Other retrofit families. Loading beyond what my load model represents.

Every boundary needs a reason: the material data I have, the assumption my model makes, the loading my code provisions cover. “I didn’t have time” is not a reason. “The model assumes X, which stops holding past Y” is.

Your Scope Paragraph

Four parts. One short paragraph. No tool names unless the tool sets the boundary.

1. In: My work covers  
2. Out: It does not cover  
3. Why here: The boundary is here because  
4. What sets it: The evidence or assumption behind that boundary is  

Test: could a committee member push on any one of your boundaries and find you don’t have a reason? Then that boundary isn’t designed yet.
