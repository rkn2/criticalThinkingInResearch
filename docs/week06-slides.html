Telling a Research Story

Putting everything we have built into an order an audience can follow

Warm-up: Your Key Finding

Write your current key finding in one sentence. Read it to yourself.

As you read, ask yourself one thing:

Is this something I learned, or something I did?

Part 1: Lead With What You Learned

We Are Trained to Build Up to the Result

Papers teach us to establish credibility first: background, then methods, then, finally, the result. A listener stops paying attention long before they get there.

How we are trained
Background
Supporting details
Result
What a listener needs
Result
Why it matters
Supporting details

Invert the pyramid. Start with the big picture, then fill in the details.

After Somerville & Hassol (2011), “Communicating the science of climate change,” Physics Today, via Shorr

Say What the Detail Means

Every technical detail is true. The audience needs to hear what it means.

The detail

“Excitation was restricted to a femtoliter volume.”

“I’m building an annotated corpus.”

“I ran 200 nonlinear analyses.”

What it means

“We eliminated a major source of noise.”

“I’m teaching a computer what to look for.”

“?”

If you can’t fill in the right-hand side for the third one, the 200 analyses are what you did. They aren’t yet what you found.

Part 2: The Story Template

Research Fits a Story Template

Good stories have clear stakes, hard obstacles, an exciting solution, and a resolution. So does good research.

GoalThe big problem you’re trying to solve
ObstacleWhy it’s hard, and why it hasn’t been done
ApproachWhat you do that gets past the obstacle
ResultWhat you found (so far)
BenefitHow that result moves the original goal forward

The elements are a checklist. A missing one is a hole in your talk.

“We Want ___, But ___”

Chain the obstacles. Each obstacle you solve becomes the next, more specific goal. Here is how Radiolab tells the story of Fritz Haber:

GoalThe world couldn’t feed a growing population.
ObstacleWe couldn’t grow enough food, because plants need nitrogen.
ObstacleThere’s plenty of nitrogen in the air, but not in a usable form.
ObstacleIt isn’t usable because nitrogen clings tightly to itself.
ApproachHaber found a way, using temperature and pressure…
Result…to turn nitrogen gas into ammonia.
BenefitThat discovery helps feed billions of people.

The Same Chain, in Our Work

Built from the Week 2 example: Kallas & Napolitano (2025), Int. J. Disaster Risk Reduction

GoalAfter a blast, responders need to know which historic buildings to check first.
ObstacleJudging vulnerability means surveying every facade by hand.
ObstacleThe attributes a field survey records may not be the ones that drive damage.
ApproachExtract geometric attributes from 3D models and test whether they predict damage better.
ResultThis is the line your committee is waiting for.
BenefitResponders can prioritize without measuring every facade.

Notice where your earlier work lands: Goal is your Step 3 (why anyone cares). The last Obstacle is your research question. Approach is your hypothesis and how you test it. Result is the finding, inside your scope.

Part 3: The Storymap

Stories Move in Two Directions

Down into detail and back up to the big picture. Left to right from problem to solution.

Big picture
GoalWhat we want is not what we have
BenefitA better world
Detail
ObstacleHere’s how we see the problem
ApproachHere’s how we try to solve it
ResultWhat we found
Problem
Solution

A talk follows a U-shape: start at the goal, go down through the obstacles, across your approach, up through your results, and finish at the benefit.

A Broken Path Loses the Audience

The most common failure: you stay on the bottom row. The talk is all approach, with no goal it serves and no benefit it delivers.

Big picture
Goal 
Benefit 
Detail
Obstacle 
Approach“Here’s what I did”
Result 

That is the “here’s what I did” talk instead of the “here’s what I learned” talk. It is the pattern this class exists to fix.

To Fill a Gap, Ask the Question

Every element has a question that pulls it out of you. Your committee will ask these if you don’t.

GoalWhy do you want to solve that problem?
ObstacleWhy is that hard? Why hasn’t it been done?
ApproachWhat do you do? What problem does that solve?
ResultWhat happened when you did that?
BenefitWhat does that result make possible?

Name Your Obstacle Precisely

“Nobody has done this” is not an obstacle. Why don’t we know the answer yet?

Lack of data: no one has collected it, or not enough of it

Lack of resolution: the data can’t show what is happening

Inconsistent findings: plenty of data, but it disagrees

Lack of accuracy: we don’t trust the data or the models we have

Lack of resources: possible in theory, impractical in time or computing

Lack of theory: the models are empirical and don’t transfer

Each kind of obstacle calls for a different approach. The more precisely you name it, the more obviously your approach fits it.

Adapted from Judith A. Swan, Princeton Writing Program, “Motives in Research”

Workshop

Workshop: Build Your Storymap

Fill in all five for your own research. One or two sentences each. Start from the pieces you already have.

Goal: What’s at stake? What happens if you’re wildly successful?
Obstacle: Why hasn’t this been solved? Which kind of obstacle is it?
Approach: What do you do that gets past it?
Result: What have you found so far?
Benefit: How does that result move the goal forward?

Write.

If Result is empty, write what you expect to find and mark it as expected. That gap is your plan for the next six weeks.

Workshop: Half-Life Your Message

Tell your story to a partner. Then tell it again in half the time. Then again.

60s
30s
15s
8s

Each round forces you to choose what survives. Whatever is left at 8 seconds is your key finding.

The goal is discovery, not a perfect soundbite. Notice what you cut first and what you refused to cut.
