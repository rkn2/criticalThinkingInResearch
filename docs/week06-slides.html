Warm-up: Your Key Finding

Write your current key finding in one sentence. Read it to yourself.

As you read, ask yourself one thing:

Is this something I learned, or something I did?

Part 1: Lead With What You Learned

We Are Trained to Build Up to the Result

Papers teach us to establish credibility first: background, then methods, then, finally, the result. A listener stops paying attention long before they get there.

How we are trained
Background
Supporting details
Result
What a listener needs
Result
Why it matters
Supporting details

Invert the pyramid. Start with the big picture, then fill in the details.

After Somerville & Hassol (2011), “Communicating the science of climate change,” Physics Today, via Shorr

Say What the Detail Means

Every technical detail is true. The audience needs to hear what it means.

The detail

“Excitation was restricted to a femtoliter volume.”

“I’m building an annotated corpus.”

“I ran 200 nonlinear analyses.”

What it means

“We eliminated a major source of noise.”

“I’m teaching a computer what to look for.”

“?”

If you can’t fill in the right-hand side for the third one, the 200 analyses are what you did. They aren’t yet what you found.

Part 2: The Story Template

Research Fits a Story Template

Good stories have clear stakes, hard obstacles, an exciting solution, and a resolution. So does good research.

GoalThe big problem you’re trying to solve
ObstacleWhy it’s hard, and why it hasn’t been done
ApproachWhat you do that gets past the obstacle
ResultWhat you found (so far)
BenefitHow that result moves the original goal forward

The elements are a checklist. A missing one is a hole in your talk.

“We Want ___, But ___”

Chain the obstacles. Each obstacle you solve becomes the next, more specific goal. Here is how Radiolab tells the story of Fritz Haber:

GoalThe world couldn’t feed a growing population.
ObstacleWe couldn’t grow enough food, because plants need nitrogen.
ObstacleThere’s plenty of nitrogen in the air, but not in a usable form.
ObstacleIt isn’t usable because nitrogen clings tightly to itself.
ApproachHaber found a way, using temperature and pressure…
Result…to turn nitrogen gas into ammonia.
BenefitThat discovery helps feed billions of people.

The Same Chain, in Our Work

Built from the research question and hypotheses we scoped last week

GoalHistoric brick buildings in tornado country need retrofits that don’t destroy what makes them historic.
ObstacleBut we don’t know the structural limits of retrofitting historic URM against tornadoes, or how those limits interact with preservation. [research question]
ObstacleBut in these buildings the floor joists just sit in pockets in the brick, with no anchors. A retrofit has to tie them to a weak lime-mortar wall, and no one knows which part of that tie gives first. [hypotheses]
ApproachTest whether bond (A), strip strength (B), or the masonry itself (C) governs, with the design efficiency factor and an FE model.
ResultThis is the line your committee is waiting for.
BenefitIf bond governs, a lighter intervention is structurally optimal, which favors preservation.

Notice where your earlier work lands: Goal is your Step 3 (why anyone cares). The first Obstacle is your research question. The last Obstacle sets up your hypotheses, and Approach is how you tell them apart. Result and Benefit have to stay inside your scope.

Part 3: The Storymap

Stories Move in Two Directions

Down into detail and back up to the big picture. Left to right from problem to solution.

Big picture
GoalWhat we want is not what we have
BenefitA better world
Detail
ObstacleHere’s how we see the problem
ApproachHere’s how we try to solve it
ResultWhat we found
Problem
Solution

A talk follows a U-shape: start at the goal, go down through the obstacles, across your approach, up through your results, and finish at the benefit.

A Broken Path Loses the Audience

The most common failure: you stay on the bottom row. The talk is all approach, with no goal it serves and no benefit it delivers.

Big picture
Goal 
Benefit 
Detail
Obstacle 
Approach“Here’s what I did”
Result 

That is the “here’s what I did” talk instead of the “here’s what I learned” talk. It is the pattern this class exists to fix.

To Fill a Gap, Ask the Question

Every element has a question that pulls it out of you. Your committee will ask these if you don’t.

GoalWhy do you want to solve that problem?
ObstacleWhy is that hard? Why hasn’t it been done?
ApproachWhat do you do? What problem does that solve?
ResultWhat happened when you did that?
BenefitWhat does that result make possible?

Name Your Obstacle Precisely

“Nobody has done this” is not an obstacle. Why don’t we know the answer yet?

Lack of data: no one has collected it, or not enough of it

Lack of resolution: the data can’t show what is happening

Inconsistent findings: plenty of data, but it disagrees

Lack of accuracy: we don’t trust the data or the models we have

Lack of resources: possible in theory, impractical in time or computing

Lack of theory: the models are empirical and don’t transfer

Each kind of obstacle calls for a different approach. The more precisely you name it, the more obviously your approach fits it.

Adapted from Judith A. Swan, Princeton Writing Program, “Motives in Research”

Workshop

Workshop: Build Your Storymap

Fill in all five for your own research. One or two sentences each. Start from the pieces you already have.

Goal: What’s at stake? What happens if you’re wildly successful?
Obstacle: Why hasn’t this been solved? Which kind of obstacle is it?
Approach: Which hypotheses are you testing, and how will you tell them apart?
Result: What have you found so far, inside your scope?
Benefit: How does that result move the goal forward?

Write.

If Result is empty, write what you expect to find and mark it as expected. That gap is your plan for the next six weeks.

Workshop: Half-Life Your Message

Tell your story to a partner. Then tell it again in half the time. Then again.

60s
30s
15s
8s

Each round forces you to choose what survives. Whatever is left at 8 seconds is your key finding.

The goal is discovery, not a perfect soundbite. Notice what you cut first and what you refused to cut.

Part 4: An Example Talk

A 4-Minute Talk, Annotated

Next: six slides of a short talk on tornado damage to historic brick in Mayfield, KY. The setting is real; the numbers are made up for teaching. The talk fills the right of each slide. The panel on the left shows where it sits on the U, and why it is built that way.

Notice the left arm. It is not one Obstacle box, it is a staircase: each “but” digs one level deeper into the goal, the way Haber went from feeding the world down to nitrogen’s chemical bond. As on slide 7, the first “but” is your research question and the next one sets up your hypotheses.

Meta: Title slide

Invert the pyramid. The title is the result, not the topic. Not “Feature Importance for Tornado Damage.”

If the committee stops listening after this slide, they still leave with the finding.

No tool names. Nobody cares which classifier yet.

About 10 seconds

Tall Parapets, Not Just Distance, Marked Which Historic Brick Buildings a Tornado Damaged

Lessons from one EF4 track through Mayfield, Kentucky

Example talk: Mayfield setting, illustrative numbers1 / 6
Meta: Goal

Goal = your Step 3. It names a reader who is not the advisor: a town preservation officer.

One slide. No literature review. The stakes are concrete: a budget and a choice.

Top-left of the U: big picture first.

About 30 seconds

A Town Can’t Retrofit Every Historic Building. Which Ones First?

  • Downtown Mayfield had about a hundred historic brick buildings
  • The 2021 tornado destroyed many of them, including the courthouse square
  • A preservation office can afford to inspect and retrofit a handful a year

We want a way to pick that handful before the next storm.

Example talk: Mayfield setting, illustrative numbers2 / 6
Meta: Obstacles, digging in

The goal carries over from the last slide, so the first “but” is clearly a problem with that goal.

Same chain as slide 7. The first “but” is the research question. The next one digs deeper and sets up the hypotheses.

Named precisely: lack of data, then two causes tangled together. Not “nobody has done this.”

About 60 seconds

Why We Can’t Just Pick

GoalPick which historic brick buildings to retrofit first.
ObstacleBut we don’t know which features of a historic brick building make tornado damage more likely. Tornadoes rarely hit these buildings, so there’s little data. [research question]
ObstacleBut even within one storm, buildings closer to the track get hit harder. A building’s features and its location are tangled together. [hypotheses]

Research question: within one tornado, which building features go with more damage, once you account for distance?

Example talk: Mayfield setting, illustrative numbers3 / 6
Meta: Approach

Approach = competing hypotheses (Week 4), plus the comparison that tells them apart.

C is named even though this data can’t test it. That is scope (Week 5), stated up front.

Bottom of the U, and only one slide. The broken path spends the whole talk here.

About 60 seconds

One Track, Three Explanations, One Test

About 80 historic brick buildings along a single EF4 track, each surveyed after the storm.

A: GeometryTall parapets and big wall openings catch the wind and fail first.
B: LocationIt’s just how close the building was to the track.
C: Hidden conditionMortar and roof anchorage decide it. Not visible from the street.

The test: compare tall and short parapets at the same distance. If B is right, the gap disappears.

Example talk: Mayfield setting, illustrative numbers4 / 6
Meta: Result

The title says what the chart means. The audience reads the answer, then checks it against the bars.

Say what the detail means: the line under the chart tells you which hypothesis it supports.

Inside the scope: one track, about 80 buildings. Climbing the right arm of the U.

About 60 seconds

Tall-Parapet Buildings Were Damaged More at Every Distance

Closer meant more damage, but distance doesn’t erase the parapet gap. That favors A over B. C is still open.

Example talk: Mayfield setting, illustrative numbers5 / 6
Meta: Benefit

Back up to the big picture. The Benefit answers the Goal slide’s question: which ones first.

The boundary is drawn before the committee asks (Week 5). The benefit stays inside the scope.

The untested hypothesis becomes the next step.

About 30 seconds

A Street-Level First Pass: Inspect Tall Parapets First

A preservation office can flag parapet height from the sidewalk or from aerial photos, then spend its inspection budget on those buildings.

What this does not tell you

How to retrofit. How to rank two buildings with the same parapet. Whether it holds beyond one EF4 track.

Next: inspect the connections on the flagged buildings to test C.

Example talk: Mayfield setting, illustrative numbers6 / 6

Check the Example Against the Storymap

GoalPick which historic brick buildings to retrofit first (30 s)
ObstaclesBut which features matter? [research question] → But features and distance are tangled [hypotheses] (60 s)
ApproachHypotheses A, B, C, and parapets compared at equal distance (60 s)
ResultThe parapet gap holds at every distance (60 s)
BenefitA street-level screen, with its limits stated (30 s)

The 8-second version: “In one tornado, tall parapets marked the historic brick buildings that got damaged, even at the same distance from the track.”

The broken-path version of this talk is six slides of data cleaning and model tuning.
