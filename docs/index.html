Phase 1: The Question — Weeks 1–4
Week 1 What does a good PhD look like? Aug 26
Done

What distinguishes a strong PhD graduate from someone who just finished? Problem solvers, not stacks of papers. People who know how to ask questions, not just do technical work. The PhD is training you to think independently — to identify a problem, design a way to investigate it, and communicate what you found.

Discussion

What does "good enough" look like at the end? What are you optimizing for — and what are you not?

Week 2 Why does anyone care? Sep 2
This week

Last week: what makes a good PhD. This week: the method for turning a topic into a question worth asking. Plus a first conversation about where the PhD leads — career paths shape who your audience is and what counts as a result.

No pre-reading. Heilmeier's Catechism handed out in session as a reference sheet.

Concept — Tasks vs. questions + the three-step formula (15 min)

The core distinction: "I'm using ABAQUS to model walls" is a task. "Can fire-cut joist retrofits resist EF3 uplift without compromising reversibility?" is a question. A task has no wrong answer. A question can be wrong — and that's what makes it research.

The Craft of Research formula (Booth et al., 3.4.1–3.4.3):

1. Name your topic: "I am studying ___."
2. Add your question: "...because I want to find out ___."
3. Answer So What?: "...in order to help my reader understand ___."

Worked example (Kallas & Napolitano blast paper): I am studying the influence of 3D geometric attributes on blast vulnerability of historic masonry buildings... because I want to find out whether geometric features from 3D models predict damage better than field-survey attributes... in order to help post-disaster responders understand which buildings to prioritize without measuring every facade by hand.

Instrumental vs. expressive behavior (Rugg & Petre, Unwritten Rules, Ch. 3): A student reading six key texts = instrumental work. A student reading the same irrelevant page in a state of panic = expressive work. If you can fill in all three blanks above, your work is instrumental. If you can only fill in step 1, you might be busy but not aimed.

Workshop — Write your three-step sentence (20 min)

Each student writes their own version of the three-step formula. Rules:

• Maximum 3 research questions, each with all three steps
• No tool names in step 1 or step 3
• Step 2 must be something you genuinely don't know yet
• Step 3 must name a reader who is not your advisor

Write for 10 min, then each reads their best one aloud.

Week 3 What are you NOT doing? Sep 9

Scope is not a limitation — it is a design decision. Claiming EF5 when your results apply to EF2-3 is not ambitious, it is indefensible. Honest scope makes your actual contribution stronger.

Workshop

Write your scope paragraph. What is in. What is out. Why the boundary is where it is.

Reading for next week

Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science" (1974, ~5 pp)

Week 4 What does your tool compute? Sep 16
Deliverable

Your model is not your contribution. Your model is an instrument. You need to know what it measures, how it measures it, and where it breaks.

Workshop

For each model or tool you use, write: (1) what question it answers, (2) what calculation it performs, (3) what it assumes, (4) where it breaks.

Reading for next week

Whitesides, "Writing a Paper" (Adv. Materials 2004, ~4 pp)

Phase 2: The Investigation — Weeks 5–8
Week 5 Designing computational experiments Sep 23

You do not "run the model." You design experiments. An experiment has a hypothesis, controlled variables, a measurable outcome, and a criterion for what counts as support or refutation.

Workshop

Design 3 computational experiments. For each: state the hypothesis, what you vary, what you hold constant, what you measure, and what result would change your conclusion.

Week 6 What counts as evidence? Sep 30

The difference between "my model ran" and "my results support the hypothesis." A converged simulation is not a finding. Evidence is a result that distinguishes between your hypothesis and an alternative.

Workshop

Take one result you already have. Write: (1) what it is, (2) what it supports, (3) what alternative it does NOT rule out, (4) what would rule that alternative out.

Week 7 Validation is not confidence Oct 7
Deliverable

Validation requires you to state in advance: what you compare against, what metric you use, what threshold counts as agreement, and what you do if it fails. Verification ≠ validation.

Workshop

Write your validation plan. For each claim: what is the evidence, and what would make you abandon the claim?

Deliverable due

Experimental design + validation plan.

Week 8 Midpoint presentation Oct 14

Each student gives a 10-minute presentation: Research question → method → preliminary results → what is next. First dry run of the December narrative.

~10 min presentation + ~7 min feedback per student.

Phase 3: The Story — Weeks 9–14
Week 9 What did you find? Oct 21

If you cannot state your finding in one sentence without naming a software package, you have not yet found anything.

Workshop

Write your results as one-sentence findings. Rank them: which is the headline?

Week 10 Making your results visible Oct 28

Every figure must have a point. If you cannot write a one-sentence caption that states what the reader should see, the figure is not ready.

Workshop

Make a figure that makes your most important finding immediately clear. Write the one-sentence caption.

Week 11 Narrative arc and presentation structure Nov 4
Deliverable

The committee needs: (1) what problem, (2) how you're solving it, (3) what you found, (4) what's next. If more than 3 slides are before your first result, cut.

Workshop

Build your December presentation outline. Slide-by-slide, one sentence per slide. 20–25 minutes total.

Deliverable due

Presentation outline (slide-by-slide).
