Answering the bottom question is what lets you work on the step above it. That is why the talk climbs in that order.

The audience hears each “but” twice, once as a problem and once with your answer.

Some talks have one research question and some have two or three

A “but” becomes a research question when your own work can answer it. If someone else makes the call, like a town, an owner or a code committee, it goes in the benefit.

One

The talk goes down the staircase, answers one question, then ends with the benefit. The Week 6 example talk was like this.

Two or three

Answer the bottom question first, then the next one up. Each gets an approach slide and a result slide.

A research question names the variables, the physics and the competing explanation

The Week 6 question is clear and casual. It says what the observer does, and not what the building does. Three moves make it a scientific question.

MoveMayfield
1. Name the variablesParapet height, storefront opening ratio and wall-to-floor anchorage, against damage state.
2. Name the physicsA tall parapet sticks up into the fastest wind. A big storefront opening lets wind pressurize the inside, which adds to roof uplift and pushes the walls outward. Walls not anchored to the floors have no path to carry that load down.
3. Name the competing explanationDistance to the track, standing in for local wind speed. Closer buildings saw higher pressures whatever their features.

There is more than one good way to write the same research question

Week 6, casualWhich features you can see from the street go with more tornado damage in historic brick buildings?
Version 1, variables first

To what extent do parapet height, storefront opening ratio and wall-to-floor anchorage explain tornado damage to historic unreinforced masonry buildings beyond what local wind speed explains?

Version 2, mechanism first

Which load-path weaknesses visible on the façade of historic unreinforced masonry buildings explain tornado damage beyond local wind speed?

Both are good research questions. Version 1 lists what gets measured. Version 2 is shorter and leaves the variables for the approach slide. The example talk uses version 1.

RQ1: approach

Competing hypotheses (Week 4) and the comparison that tells them apart, on one slide.

C is named even though this data can’t test it. It comes back as the next step in the benefit.

Three explanations for the damage predict different patterns

About 80 historic brick buildings along one EF4 track, each surveyed after the storm.

A: GeometryA tall parapet sticks up into the fastest wind. A big storefront opening lets wind pressurize the inside, adding to roof uplift and pushing the walls outward.
B: Local wind speedDistance to the track stands in for wind speed. Closer buildings saw higher pressures, whatever their features.
C: Hidden conditionMortar and wall-to-floor ties decide whether the walls can carry the load down to the floors. They are hard to see from the street.

The test. Compare tall and short parapets at the same distance from the track. If B is right, the gap disappears.

RQ1: result

The title states the answer to RQ1. The line under the chart says which hypothesis it supports.

Tall-parapet buildings were damaged more at every distance from the track

Closer meant more damage, and the parapet gap stays at every distance. That favors A over B. C is still open.

RQ2: approach

One approach slide per research question. No new staircase, no second U.

The comparison is chosen so a mismatch would show up in a specific feature.

Volunteers and an engineer rated the same 80 buildings from the sidewalk

  • Each volunteer got a one-page sheet with photos of the three features from slide 3 and marked each one yes or no
  • An engineer rated the same buildings with the same sheet
  • For each feature we count how often the volunteer and the engineer agreed

If volunteers can’t see a feature, agreement on that feature drops toward chance.

Benefit, back at the goal

The benefit answers the goal slide’s question with what the two questions found, and claims no more than that.

The open hypothesis (C, the wall ties) becomes the next step.

A town could sort its historic buildings from the sidewalk before paying for any assessment

Volunteers flag tall parapets, which they rate about as well as an engineer. The engineering budget goes to those buildings first, starting with their wall ties.

What this does not tell you

How to retrofit. How to rank two buildings with the same features. Whether it holds beyond one EF4 track.

Part 3: Your Talk

Workshop: turn your staircase into a strip of slide titles

  1. Copy the staircase from your Oct 22 storymap.
  2. Mark each “but” your own work can answer.
  3. Run the three moves on your bottom research question. Name the variables, the physics and the competing explanation.
  4. Write your research questions slide, with the question that answers the bottom “but” first.
  5. Write the title of every slide in your December talk as a full sentence, in order.

Hot seat: the others read only your titles

  • Where do they lose the thread?
  • Does every “but” from the way down get an answer on the way up, or a place in the benefit?
  • Does the benefit claim more than the questions answered?
  • Does the research questions slide come back after each result?
