What Don’t You Know?

Step 2 of your three-step sentence is your research question. This week, make it a good one.

A research question identifies what you don’t know. It defines a territory with a range of possible answers.

Research question“What are the structural limits of retrofitting historic URM against tornado loading, and how do those limits interact with preservation constraints?”
Opens a door. Multiple answers are possible. You don’t know which one is right.
Not a research question“I am optimizing retrofit strategies for fire-cut joist connections using ABAQUS.”
That’s a task. It tells me what you’re doing, not what you’re trying to find out.

Is your question real? Three tests

  1. Does it name what you don’t know? If you already know the answer, it’s not a question. It’s a demonstration.
  2. Does it have more than one possible answer? “Does CFRP improve capacity?” has only one plausible answer (yes). The interesting question is how much, where it stops helping, or what governs the limit.
  3. Can you imagine evidence that would change the answer? If no evidence could change your mind, you’re not asking. You’re asserting.

Common problems with Step 2

Too broad

“I want to find out about tornado damage to buildings.” That’s a topic. What specifically do you not know?

Foregone conclusion

“I want to find out whether my retrofit improves structural performance.” Of course it does. That’s why it’s called a retrofit. What is the interesting question hiding behind it?

Tool-centric

“I want to find out what DIANA predicts for this loading case.” DIANA doesn’t predict. You do. What question are you asking the instrument?

Restating Step 1

“I want to find out about the influence of geometric attributes on vulnerability.” That’s Step 1 with “find out” pasted in front. What do you genuinely not know?
