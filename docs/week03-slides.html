Last time you wrote three-step sentences. Step 2 — “because I want to find out ___” — is your research question. But we haven’t named it that way yet, or tested whether it’s actually a question worth asking.

Part 1: The Question

What Is a Research Question?

A research question identifies what you don’t know. It defines a territory with a range of possible answers.

Research question

“What are the structural limits of retrofitting historic URM against tornado loading, and how do those limits interact with preservation constraints?”

Opens a door. Multiple answers are possible. You don’t know which one is right.

Not a research question

“I am optimizing retrofit strategies for fire-cut joist connections using ABAQUS.”

That’s a task. It tells me what you’re doing, not what you’re trying to find out.

Is Your Question Real?

Three tests:

  • Does it name what you don’t know? If you already know the answer, it’s not a question — it’s a demonstration.
  • Does it have more than one possible answer? “Does CFRP improve capacity?” has only one plausible answer (yes). The interesting question is how much, where it stops helping, or what governs the limit.
  • Can you imagine evidence that would change the answer? If no evidence could change your mind, you’re not asking — you’re asserting.

Common Problems with Step 2

Too broad

“I want to find out about tornado damage to buildings.”

That’s a topic, not a question. What about tornado damage? What specifically do you not know?

Foregone conclusion

“I want to find out whether my retrofit improves structural performance.”

Of course it does. That’s why it’s called a retrofit. What is the interesting question hiding behind this?

Tool-centric

“I want to find out what DIANA predicts for this loading case.”

DIANA doesn’t predict. You do. DIANA is the instrument. What question are you asking it?

Restating Step 1

“I want to find out about the influence of geometric attributes on vulnerability.”

That’s Step 1 again with “find out” pasted in front. What do you genuinely not know?

Workshop: Sharpen Your Question

Pull up the three-step sentence you wrote last time. Look at Step 2.

2. because I want to find out  
  • Rewrite it as a standalone question. Does it pass all three tests?
  • If it’s too broad, narrow it. What specific thing do you not know?
  • If it’s a foregone conclusion, find the interesting question hiding behind it.
  • If it names a tool, remove the tool. What question are you asking with the tool?

Write for 5 minutes, then each reads theirs aloud.

Part 2: The Bet

From Question to Hypothesis

A research question opens a door. A hypothesis walks through it.

Research question

What you’re trying to find out.

It defines the territory. It has a range of possible answers. You don’t know which one is right.

Hypothesis

A proposed answer — a specific, testable bet.

It picks one door, walks through it, and says: “I think this is what’s on the other side.” It can be wrong.

Worked Example

Retrofitting historic masonry against tornadoes

Research question

What are the structural limits of retrofitting historic URM against tornado loading, and how do those limits interact with preservation constraints?

Hypothesis

On historic lime-mortar brick, the bond between the composite strip and the wall — not the strip’s own strength — limits what any surface-mounted retrofit can deliver. This limit favors preservation, because a lighter intervention is structurally optimal.

The question had many possible answers. The hypothesis picked one and committed to it.

What Would Make You Wrong?

If you can’t answer this, you don’t have a hypothesis — you have a hope.

The hypothesis holds if

The ACI reinforcement efficiency factor collapses to its floor on low-strength masonry, AND the FE model shows that connection layout — not strip strength — governs capacity.

The hypothesis fails if

κv does not collapse on low-f′m masonry, OR the FE model shows that adding more fibre proportionally increases capacity.

Both outcomes are real possibilities. That’s what makes it research, not a demonstration.

These Are Not Hypotheses

Unfalsifiable

“My model will provide useful results.”

What result would make you say it didn’t?

A task in disguise

“I will use ABAQUS to simulate the wall.”

That’s a plan. What do you expect to find?

Foregone conclusion

“The retrofitted wall will perform better.”

Of course it does. What is the interesting claim?

Too vague

“Building characteristics affect damage.”

Which characteristics? Which damage? Under what conditions?

Why You Need More Than One

Chamberlin, “The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses” (1890)

The moment one has offered an original explanation for a phenomenon which seems satisfactory, that moment affection for his intellectual child springs into existence… There is an unconscious selection and magnifying of the phenomena that fall into harmony with the theory and support it, and an unconscious neglect of those that fail of coincidence. — T. C. Chamberlin, Science (1890)

If you hold only one hypothesis, you design experiments to confirm it. If you hold two competing hypotheses, you design experiments to distinguish between them. That is a different — and better — kind of research.

Workshop: What’s Your Bet?

Start from the research question you just sharpened.

  • Write one hypothesis — a proposed answer. Specific enough to be wrong.
  • Write what would make you wrong — what evidence would kill it?
  • Write a competing hypothesis — a different answer to the same question. What experiment would distinguish between them?

Write for 10 minutes. Then each reads theirs aloud.
