Tasks vs. Questions

These are not research questions yet. This is a way of thinking about your work: everything you do is either a task or aimed at answering a question. Both are real — but you need to know which is which.

Task

Something you do. It has steps and a completion state, but no wrong answer.

“I'm using ABAQUS to model walls.”

Question

Something you're trying to find out. It can be wrong — and that's what makes it research.

“Can fire-cut joist retrofits resist EF3 uplift without compromising reversibility?”

Write it down (jargon is fine)

What tasks did you do this week? What questions were you answering?

The Three-Step Formula

Booth et al., The Craft of Research, 3.4.1–3.4.3

1. I am studying  
2. because I want to find out  
3. in order to help my reader understand  
  • Step 1 names your topic — use action nouns (causes, influence, performance)
  • Step 2 turns it into a question — what you don't know
  • Step 3 answers So what? — why anyone outside your lab should care

Worked Example

Kallas & Napolitano (2025), Int. J. Disaster Risk Reduction

1. I am studying the influence of 3D geometric attributes on blast vulnerability of historic masonry buildings…

2. because I want to find out whether geometric features extracted from 3D models predict damage better than traditional field-survey attributes…

3. in order to help post-disaster responders understand which buildings to prioritize without needing to measure every facade by hand.

Instrumental vs. Expressive

Rugg & Petre, The Unwritten Rules of PhD Research, Ch. 3

Instrumental

Behavior that moves you toward your goal.

A student reading six key texts for their area.

Expressive

Behavior that demonstrates effort but produces nothing.

A student reading the same irrelevant page over and over in a state of panic.

The three-step formula is a diagnostic: if you can fill in all three blanks, your work is instrumental. If you can only fill in step 1, you might be busy — but not aimed.

Where Is the PhD Taking You?

The PhD opens several doors.
It helps to know what's behind each one.

Tenure-Line Faculty

  • Primary job: Research + grants + teaching + service
  • Evaluated on: Publications, funding, impact
  • Build during PhD: Papers, conference talks, grant experience
  • Tradeoff: Freedom vs. pressure (tenure clock)

Teaching Professor

  • Primary job: Teaching + curriculum + mentoring
  • Evaluated on: Teaching quality, student outcomes
  • Build during PhD: Teaching portfolio, pedagogy
  • Tradeoff: Stability vs. less research autonomy

Industry

  • Primary job: Applied R&D or practice
  • Evaluated on: Deliverables, clients, products
  • Build during PhD: Technical skills, internships
  • Tradeoff: Pay/balance vs. less intellectual freedom
