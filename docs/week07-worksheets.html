Walk the U, One Stop at a Time

From your Week 6 storymap to a 5-minute pitch you can build in PowerPoint

A talk follows a U: start at the Goal, dig down through the Obstacles, cross your Approach, climb up through your Result, and finish at the Benefit. These pages take you around it one stop at a time. Do them in order. Each one builds on the page before.

Every page has the same four parts

  1. The question that pulls it out. If you don’t answer it, your committee will ask it.
  2. An example from the Mayfield talk in Week 6. The setting is real; the numbers are made up for teaching.
  3. Your turn. Write in sentences, not keywords. Start from what you already have: your Step 3, research question, hypotheses, scope paragraph, and boundary map.
  4. Check before you move on. These are the questions the group will ask when you pitch. Ask them of yourself first.

The last two pages put the U back together and turn it into a slide plan: six slides, five minutes.

Stage 1 of 5 · Top left of the U

Goal: What Do We Want?

Big picture first. The problem, and who has it.

The question: Why do you want to solve that problem? What happens if you are wildly successful?
Example A preservation office in a tornado town can afford to inspect and retrofit a handful of historic brick buildings a year. We want a way to pick that handful before the next storm.
Who cares: a town preservation officer. Stakes: a budget and a choice. No literature review.

Who has this problem? Name a person or role who is not your advisor and not another researcher in your area.

What do they need that they don’t have? Something concrete: a decision, a number, a design, a warning.

Your Goal in one or two sentences. Try starting with “We want…”

Check before you move on

  • Is the goal clear, and would the person you named say “yes, that’s my problem”?
  • Does it match your Step 3 (why anyone cares)? If it changed, which one is right?
  • Could you say it without naming a method or tool? Nobody cares which model yet.
Stage 2 of 5 · Down the left arm

Obstacles: “We Want ___, But ___”

Each obstacle becomes a narrower goal. Keep digging until you hit your research question.

The question: Why is that hard? Why hasn’t it been done? Then ask it again of your answer.
Example We want to pick which historic buildings to retrofit first, but a full assessment of every building costs more than a small town can spend. [resources]
So we want a first pass from what you can see from the street, but wind design rules were written for modern buildings; nothing says which features of historic brick matter in a tornado. [theory]
Research question: Which features you can see from the street go with more tornado damage in historic brick buildings?

Name each obstacle’s kind. “Nobody has done this” is not an obstacle.

Data: no one has collected it, or not enough
Resolution: the data can’t show what is happening
Inconsistent: plenty of data, but it disagrees
Accuracy: we don’t trust the data or models
Resources: possible in theory, impractical in time or computing
Theory: models are empirical and don’t transfer

Stage 3 of 5 · Across the bottom

Approach: How Do You Tell Them Apart?

Competing hypotheses, and the one comparison that separates them. One slide, not the whole talk.

The question: What do you do? What problem does that solve? (It should solve the bottom obstacle on your staircase.)
Example About 80 historic brick buildings along one EF4 track. A, geometry: tall parapets and big openings fail first. B, location: it’s just distance from the track. C, hidden condition: mortar and roof anchorage decide it, not visible from the street.
The test: compare tall and short parapets at the same distance. If B is right, the gap disappears. C can’t be tested with this data, and the talk says so.

The comparison that tells them apart. What do you vary, what do you hold fixed, and what do you measure?

What this data can’t test. Name it now so your committee doesn’t have to.

Check before you move on

  • Are there competing hypotheses, and a way to tell them apart? A list of methods is not an approach.
  • Does each hypothesis predict something different you could actually see?
  • Could you say this on one slide? If you need four, you are on the broken “here’s what I did” path.
Stage 4 of 5 · Up the right arm

Result: What Did You Learn?

Not what you did. What it means for your hypotheses.

The question: What happened when you did that? Which hypothesis does it favor?
Example Tall-parapet buildings were damaged more at every distance: 85% vs 62% within 100 m, 41% vs 17% past 300 m. What it means: distance doesn’t erase the parapet gap, so it favors A over B. C is still open.

No result yet? Write what you expect to find and label it EXPECTED. That gap is your plan for the rest of the semester.

Say what each detail means.

The detail (what you did or saw)What it means (what you learned)
“I ran 200 nonlinear analyses.”If you can’t fill this side in, it is something you did, not something you found.

Your Result in one or two sentences. Say which hypothesis it supports and which it rules out.

If this were a slide, what would the title say? A sentence that states the finding, not a topic. “Tall-parapet buildings were damaged more at every distance,” not “Damage results.”

Check before you move on

  • Is there a result (or an expected one, labeled), or only “here’s what I did”?
  • Does it answer your research question from the bottom of the staircase?
  • Does it stay inside your scope paragraph and boundary map?
Stage 5 of 5 · Top right of the U

Benefit: Back to the Big Picture

The Benefit answers the question your Goal asked.

The question: What does that result make possible? For the person you named on the Goal page?
Example The Goal asked “which ones first?” Benefit: a preservation office can flag parapet height from the sidewalk or aerial photos and spend its inspection budget there.
What this does not tell you: how to retrofit, how to rank two buildings with the same parapet, whether it holds beyond one track. Next: inspect connections on flagged buildings to test C.

Copy your Goal here. From Stage 1, word for word.

Your Benefit: how does your Result move that Goal forward?

What this does not tell you. Draw the boundary before the committee asks. Your boundary map has these.

Next step. Often the hypothesis you couldn’t test yet.

Check before you move on

  • Does the Benefit answer the Goal? Read them one after the other, out loud to yourself.
  • Is the benefit inside your scope, or does it promise more than your Result can carry?
  • Would the person you named on the Goal page know what to do differently?

Is your path broken?

The most common failure is staying on the bottom row: the talk is all Approach, with no Goal it serves and no Benefit it delivers. Look at what you wrote above.

  • All five boxes are filled, and none of them is just a method.
  • Goal and Benefit are about the person you named, not about your model.
  • Approach is the shortest box, not the longest.

The 8-second version

Whatever is left at 8 seconds is your key finding. Example: “In one tornado, tall parapets marked the historic brick buildings that got damaged, even at the same distance from the track.”

Compare it with your warm-up sentence. What changed?

Before you build

  • Read just the six titles in order. Do they tell the story by themselves?
  • Slide 1 states the finding. If someone stops listening there, they still leave with it.
  • No slide is mostly data cleaning, model setup, or tool names.
  • Every chart has a title that says what it means, and a line saying which hypothesis it supports.
