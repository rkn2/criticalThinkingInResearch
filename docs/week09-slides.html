Warm-up: Your Key Finding

Write your current key finding in one sentence. Read it to yourself.

Then ask yourself one thing.

Could this sentence sit at the top of a slide, with the evidence for it underneath?

Part 1: Start From Your Audience

A talk is meant to move your audience from where they start to somewhere new

Every time you present, you are trying to move people from where they are to somewhere else. If you can’t say where, you can’t tell which slides help. Three goals people often start from leave that open.

“I want them to be impressed with my work.”Impressed people can still leave without knowing what to do with your result.
“I want them to understand how this works.”You can’t check whether they understood every step. Which step matters for what they decide?
“I don’t want to bias them, so I have no goal.”A committee always leaves with some opinion. Without a goal, you leave which one to chance.

A From/To table names where each person starts and where you want them to end up

Part 2: Distill Your Message

Clear slides come from two steps, deciding the message and then steering the eye to it

1. Distill your message
What should the audience take from this slide?
→
2. Align visual emphasis
Make the eye land on the evidence for it.

Most slide trouble starts at the first step. If you haven’t decided what a chart means, no design choice will show it.

A topic title leaves the audience to guess what the chart means

Illustrative numbers

Topic titleRetrofit results
One readingEvery retrofit raised the wind speed the wall could take
Another readingOnly the invasive retrofits held into the EF3 range

Both sentences are true. Only you know which one your talk needs, so the title has to say it.

A sentence title states the finding the slide shows

Says who did what, in subject, verb, object order
Makes a claim the evidence on the slide supports
Completes “Today I learned that…”
Often runs to two lines, and that’s fine
Topic titleSentence title
Model validationThe model matched the observed damage grade at 8 of 10 Mayfield walls
Mesh convergenceRefining the mesh below 50 mm changed peak stress by less than 2%
Literature reviewNo study has tested tornado retrofits on historic masonry against observed damage
Future workNext we test whether hidden strips work on two-story walls

Burying the lead puts the finding in the last bullet

Before

Hidden steel strip analysis

  • Modeled a 12 m wall in LS-DYNA
  • Applied the Mayfield wind profile
  • Strips at 1.2 m spacing
  • Ran 40 wind speeds
  • Wall failed at 128 mph, in the EF2 range
After

Hidden steel strips kept the wall standing only to 128 mph, inside the EF2 range

The setup goes in what you say, or on a backup slide for questions.

Read only the titles of your deck, in order. If the titles alone tell your story, the deck works.

Part 3: Steer the Eye

Three tools line up what the eye sees with what the title says

1. Layer the slideBackground, data, and emphasis, from quietest to loudest.
2. Match the form to the argumentThe chart type fits the comparison, and color fits how the data relate.
3. Make the key check easyWhatever the audience has to do to verify your title should take the least effort.

Graying out the context lets the finding come forward

Before · Retrofit results
After · Only the invasive retrofits held into the EF3 range

Background (axes, grid, labels) in light gray. Data in a quiet color. Emphasis in one strong color on the marks the title is about. The numbers sit on the bars, so the legend can go.

Illustrative numbers

Color should follow how the data relate to each other

QualitativeSeparate categories with no order. Hues spaced evenly so none looks more important.
AccentOne category is the point. Everything else is gray.
SequentialMore or less of one thing. One hue, light to dark.
DivergentAbove or below a meaningful middle. Two hues with gray at the center.

A rainbow scale invents boundaries the data don’t have, and readers with color blindness lose it. Pick the chart type the same way, from the comparison the title makes (a ranking, a change over time, parts of a whole, or one measurement against another).

Put the two things your title compares side by side, on one axis

Before · Model validation

Wall number on the x-axis. To check a wall, find it in both panels and compare bar heights.

After · The model matched the observed damage at 8 of 10 walls

model and observed agree   they disagree

Find the key task, what the audience has to do to check your title. Emphasize those marks, gray out the rest, keep the eye’s path short, and label directly so nobody has to memorize a legend.

Squint test. Squint at the slide. What you can still see should be the finding.

Workshop 2

Retitle your Oct 22 pitch deck

  • Open your pitch deck or its printout.
  • For each slide, write the current title, then a sentence title.
  • Mark whether the evidence on the slide shows that sentence. If it doesn’t, the title or the slide has to change.
  • Read your new titles in order. Do they walk the U from Week 6, goal down to the research question and back up to the benefit?

Workshop 3

Redesign one figure by hand

  • Pick the figure your audience is most likely to stop on.
  • Write its sentence title and the key task.
  • Sketch it again with the three tools. Decide what goes gray, what gets the one strong color, and what sits next to what.
  • Swap with a partner. They squint and tell you what they see first.
