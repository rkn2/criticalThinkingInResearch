Building a Talk

The Story Template
Ardon Shorr, “Telling Research Stories,” Princeton (2020)
Goal: the big problem you’re trying to solve
Obstacle: why it’s hard, and why it hasn’t been done
Approach: what you do that gets past the obstacle
Result: what you found (so far)
Benefit: how that result moves the goal forward

The elements are a checklist. A missing one is a hole in your talk. To fill a gap, ask its question: Why solve that? Why is it hard? What do you do? What happened? What does that make possible?

Week 6 slides
“We Want ___, But ___”

Chain the obstacles. Each “but” becomes the next, narrower goal. Keep asking until you reach your research question.

Goal: The world couldn’t feed a growing population.
But we couldn’t grow enough food, because plants need nitrogen.
But the nitrogen in the air isn’t in a form plants can use.
But it won’t change form, because nitrogen clings tightly to itself.
Approach: Haber used temperature and pressure… Result: …to turn nitrogen gas into ammonia.

Name each obstacle precisely. “Nobody has done this” is not an obstacle. Which kind is it?

  • Lack of data: not collected, or not enough
  • Lack of resolution: the data can’t show what’s happening
  • Inconsistent findings: plenty of data, but it disagrees
  • Lack of accuracy: we don’t trust the data or models
  • Lack of resources: possible in theory, impractical in time or computing
  • Lack of theory: the models are empirical and don’t transfer
Haber chain via Radiolab and Shorr; obstacle types after Judith A. Swan, “Motives in Research”
The U-Shape, and the Broken Path

A talk starts at the goal (big picture), goes down through the obstacles, across your approach (detail), up through your result, and finishes at the benefit (big picture again).

The most common failure stays on the bottom row: all approach, no goal it serves, no benefit it delivers. That is the “here’s what I did” talk instead of the “here’s what I learned” talk.

Annotated 5-minute example pitch, Week 6 slides 14–21
Lead With What You Learned
After Somerville & Hassol (2011), Physics Today; Aurbach et al. (2018)
  • Invert the pyramid. Papers build up to the result. A listener needs the result first, then why it matters, then the details.
  • Say what the detail means. Every figure and number gets a sentence that tells the audience what to take from it. A slide title can state the finding.
  • Half-life your message. Tell it in 60 seconds, then 30, 15, and 8. Whatever survives at 8 seconds is your key finding.

Giving Feedback on a Talk

Six Questions, in Storymap Terms
  • Is the goal clear, and who cares?
  • Do the “but”s dig down to a research question?
  • Are there competing hypotheses, and a way to tell them apart?
  • Is there a result, or only “here’s what I did”?
  • Does the benefit stay inside the scope?
  • Can the speaker say it in 8 seconds?

Answering Questions

Your Boundary Is Where You Show What You Know

A question about something past your line usually isn’t a request to do it. It checks whether you understand the other side. “That’s out of scope” makes the line look like where you stopped looking.

1. Reason past the line: what you’d expect there, and why.
2. Anchor the line: why your work stops here.
3. Name the cost: what it would take to cross, and that it is not part of this work.
Shows you can think
  • Predict: “I’d expect… because…”
  • Mechanism: “What changes past that point is…”
  • Literature: “Others who looked at that found…”
  • Cost: “Answering it would take…”
Signs you up for work
  • “I could add that.”
  • “That should be easy to include.”
  • “I’ll look into running that.”
  • “I haven’t had time yet.”

If a push shows a boundary has no reason, the boundary moves.

Week 5 slides
When It Really Is a Request for More Work

“You should also…” or “Before you defend, I’d want to see…” is a proposal to change your project. In the room, engage the same way: reason about it, anchor your line, name the cost. Then say you’ll think about how it fits and follow up with your advisor. Don’t agree to new work on the spot.

Questions a Committee Asks
  • What calculation does that tool actually perform?
  • Why this method and not an alternative?
  • What would make you wrong?
  • Who uses this, and under what circumstances?
  • What does your loading represent physically?
  • Is your computational strategy feasible?
  • What decisions does this support?
  • What data do you wish you had, and what do you do without it?

Checks for Each Piece

The Three-Step Sentence
Booth et al., The Craft of Research, 3.4.1–3.4.3
1. I am studying ___
2. because I want to find out ___
3. in order to help my reader understand ___

No tool names in Step 1 or Step 3. Step 2 must be something you don’t know yet. Step 3 names a reader who is not your advisor.

Week 2 slides
Is Your Research Question Real?

Three tests: it names what you don’t know; it has more than one possible answer; you can imagine evidence that would change the answer.

Common problems: too broad (a topic, not a question), a foregone conclusion (“does my retrofit help?”), tool-centric, or a restatement of Step 1.

A hypothesis with a question mark can be answered yes or no, you already expect yes, or it names the answer. Keep it as your hypothesis and work backward to the question it answers. Then list two other possible answers. If you can’t, the question is still too narrow.

Week 3 · Week 4 slides
Is It a Hypothesis?
Chamberlin, “The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses” (1890)

A hypothesis is a proposed answer, specific enough to be wrong. Write what would make you wrong. If you can’t, you have a hope, not a hypothesis. Then write a competing answer and the result that would tell the two apart.

Not hypotheses: unfalsifiable (“my model will provide useful results”), a task in disguise (“I will simulate the wall”), a foregone conclusion, or too vague (“building characteristics affect damage”).

Carry more than one. Once you like an answer, it is easy to keep tuning the model until it agrees with you.

Week 4 slides
The Scope Paragraph
In: My work covers ___
Out: It does not cover ___
Why here: The boundary is here because ___
What sets it: The evidence or assumption behind that boundary is ___

Write it once for your question and once for your hypothesis; the hypothesis boundary is usually smaller. It is set by where your evidence can tell your answer apart from the competing ones. Every “out” needs a because. “I didn’t have time” is not one.
