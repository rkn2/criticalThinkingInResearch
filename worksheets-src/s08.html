Three rules for the askers

Cut at 30 seconds

The person on the presenter’s right is timekeeper. At 30 s, say “Time.” Mid-sentence is fine. The presenter then gives the answer again in one sentence.

Make them answer

If the first sentence didn’t answer the question, say: “So is that a yes or a no?” or “So what’s the number?” Then wait.

Play your role

Each asker takes one role and asks from it; rotate roles every round. Mechanics: what is physically happening. Model: why this model, and is it right. Data and users: where the data came from, and who acts on the result. Example questions are on the Question bank page; start there, then follow up on what you hear.

Week 8 · Read first

Answer, Why, Stop

A committee question is not a cue to give a mini-lecture. It is a question. Answer it.

1. AnswerThe first sentence is the answer: yes, no, partly, a number, a name, or “I don’t know yet.”
2. WhyOne sentence of reason or evidence. One, not three.
3. StopStop talking. If they want more, they will ask. Silence after your answer is fine.
“Did you validate the model?” Rambling“So, validation is a big topic. First I set up the mesh, and I did a convergence study, and then I looked at some papers that used similar models, and the material properties came from the literature, so I think in a way, yes, it’s validated, more or less.”
35 seconds and the listener still doesn’t know.
Answer, why, stop“Partly. It matches a hand calculation within 10%, but I haven’t compared it to a physical test.”
6 seconds. If they care about the test, they will ask about it next.

A move for each kind of question

Examples use the class example from Weeks 5 and 6: composite strips on historic brick walls under tornado load. A: the bond to lime mortar controls. B: the strip’s strength controls.

They askYour moveSounds like
Yes or no
“Does the strip ever break before it debonds?”		Yes or no first, then the why.“No, not on lime mortar. The bond goes at about a third of the strip’s strength.”
What is it
“What does your load represent physically?”		Plain words, no jargon. Say what it leaves out.“The wind pressure from an EF2 to EF3 tornado on the long wall, held steady. It leaves out debris and the sudden pressure drop.”
Why this, not that
“Why not test cement mortar too?”		Name the choice and the reason. Don’t apologize.“Because on cement mortar the bond is strong, so A and B predict the same thing. Lime mortar is where they split.”
Does it generalize
“Would this work in an EF5?”		Say it’s outside your claim, and what would change.“I’m not claiming that. Above EF3 the wall fails before the strips matter, so it would need a different retrofit.”
Challenge
“Your model says the wall fails, but the real one stood.”		Agree with what’s true. Say what it means.“Right, so the model is missing some strength. My best guess is the floor tying the wall in, and I’m adding it next.”
You don’t knowSay so, then how you’d find out.“I don’t know yet. I’d get it from the mortar tests in my materials data.”
You didn’t followAsk which one they mean. Then answer.“Do you mean the strip or the bond?”

Take three seconds. Pausing to think looks confident; starting to talk before you know your answer is how rambling starts. Answer the question, not the slide. They almost never want your whole method again.

Week 8 · Question bank · For the askers

Ask Like a Committee

Pick your role for the round. Start from these, put in their words (their load, their model, their buildings), then follow up on whatever the answer leaves open.

Mechanics

What is physically happening?
  • What does that load represent physically? What does it leave out?
  • What fails first, and how do you know?
  • Why is that boundary condition right for this building?
  • Is that number big or small? Compared to what?
  • If your model is right, what would we see on the real building?
  • Where does the load go after it hits the wall?

Model

Why this model, and is it right?
  • Why this model and not a hand calculation?
  • What did you compare it to? How close was it?
  • Which one assumption, if wrong, changes your answer?
  • How much does the result move if you change the input you trust least?
  • Your model says it fails, but the real one stood. Why?
  • Can you finish this by December? What would you cut?

Data and users

Where did it come from, and who acts on it?
  • Who uses this, and what decision does it change?
  • What would an engineer or preservation officer do differently tomorrow?
  • Where did that data come from? How good is it?
  • How many buildings is that based on?
  • Is your “truth” biased? Who collected it, and how?
  • Does this hold for buildings that aren’t like yours? What data do you wish you had?

Follow-ups, any role

Use these when an answer runs long, drifts or dodges.
  • So is that a yes or a no?
  • What’s the number?
  • Can you say that in one sentence?
  • Why?
  • How do you know?
  • What result would change your mind?

Good questions are short, ask one thing, and can’t be answered by re-showing a slide. Ask why and what if, not only what.
