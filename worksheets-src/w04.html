Week 4 · Read first

From Question to Hypothesis

Separating what you don’t know from what you think the answer is, and why you need more than one

A hypothesis with a question mark

Looks like a question“Does the bond between the composite strip and lime-mortar brick govern the capacity of surface-mounted retrofits?”
You already believe the answer (bond) and are asking for “yes.”
Is a question“What governs the capacity of surface-mounted retrofits on historic lime-mortar brick?”
Bond is one possible answer. So is strip strength. So is the masonry itself.

How to tell which one you wrote. Can it be answered yes or no? Do you already expect “yes”? Does it name the answer (a specific mechanism, variable, or direction)? If any is true, you wrote a hypothesis. That’s not a failure. You’ll need it. It just isn’t the question yet.

The move: work backward

“Does bond govern capacity?” → keep it: that’s your hypothesis, bond governs → write the question it answers: “What governs capacity?” Then list two other possible answers. If you can’t, the question is still your hypothesis in different words.

What a hypothesis is

A research question opens a door. A hypothesis walks through it: a proposed answer, specific and testable. It can be wrong.

Example: possible answers to one question A. The bond between strip and wall governs, so a lighter intervention is structurally optimal, which favors preservation. B. The strip’s own strength governs: more fibre, proportionally more capacity. C. The masonry itself fails first, and the retrofit barely engages.
Pick one and commit: A is the hypothesis. B and C are its competitors.
Don’t set out to collect data in support of your belief. Ask yourself: ‘How could I tell if my belief was wrong?’ If you can’t answer that question, then you’re doing politics, not research. — Rugg & Petre, The Unwritten Rules of PhD Research, p. 215

These are not hypotheses

Unfalsifiable

“My model will provide useful results.”

A task in disguise

“I will use ABAQUS to simulate the wall.”

Foregone

“The retrofitted wall will perform better.”

Too vague

“Building characteristics affect damage.”

Week 4 · Worksheet 2

Propose an Answer, Then Try to Kill It

A hypothesis, what would make you wrong, a competitor, and the experiment between them

Your hypothesis. Pick one of your three answers. Make it specific enough to be wrong.

What would make you wrong? If you can’t answer this, you don’t have a hypothesis. You have a hope.

It holds if…It fails if…
Example Holds if the ACI reinforcement efficiency factor collapses to its floor on low-strength masonry, and the FE model shows that connection layout, not strip strength, governs capacity. Fails if it does not collapse on low-strength masonry, or the FE model shows that adding more fibre proportionally increases capacity.

A competing hypothesis. One of your other two answers, to the same question.

One experiment that distinguishes them.

The experimentIf your hypothesis is right, you’d see…If the competitor is right, you’d see…

Check before you move on

  • Your hypothesis isn’t unfalsifiable, a task in disguise, a foregone conclusion, or too vague.
  • Both hypotheses answer the same question. If one answers a different question, the pair doesn’t hold together yet.
  • The two right-hand cells say different things. If they match, the experiment can’t tell them apart.
Week 4 · Reflect

Your Intellectual Child

Why you need more than one hypothesis

The moment one has offered an original explanation for a phenomenon which seems satisfactory, that moment affection for his intellectual child springs into existence… There is an unconscious selection and magnifying of the phenomena that fall into harmony with the theory and support it, and an unconscious neglect of those that fail of coincidence. — T. C. Chamberlin, “The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses,” Science (1890)

If you hold only one hypothesis, you design experiments to confirm it. If you hold two competing hypotheses, you design experiments to distinguish between them. That is a different and better kind of research.

What is your intellectual child? What result would you be tempted to explain away?

xkcd 1838, Randall Munroe

Stir until it looks right. This is the intellectual child with a computer attached. Once you like an answer, it is easy to keep tuning the model, the mesh, or the inputs until the output agrees with you. The pile will always produce an answer. The question is whether you would have accepted a different one.

When did you last stop adjusting because the result looked right, rather than because you had a reason to stop?
